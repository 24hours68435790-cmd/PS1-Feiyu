\section{Three connected research questions}
Algorithmic scoring makes reported firm metrics consequential for capital
allocation, and once a metric is a target the firm can optimise the number
rather than the fundamentals behind it~\cite{strathern1997}.

Economics has studied this against human markets for decades: earnings
management~\cite{healy1999}, signal-jamming equilibria in which rational markets
discount inflation and wasteful inflation survives anyway~\cite{stein1989}, and
private-equity funds that appear to inflate interim returns around
fundraising~\cite{brown2019}. Machine learning arrives at the same place from
the other side: a deployed model changes the distribution it is meant to
predict~\cite{perdomo2020}, and agents respond to a learner's announced
rule~\cite{hardt2016}.

The stakeholders are the startups and funds being scored,
investors who allocate mechanically from the posted price, and an auditor who
can observe the evaluator's behaviour but not its weights.

\textbf{The strategic tension.} An AI evaluator, unlike a market, can retrain.
It need not follow the rule it announces. That is where the 2001 Nobel
foundation stops~\cite{nobel2001}: costly signals carry information when
information is unequal~\cite{spence1973}, but the receiver's rule is taken as
fixed. Two recent results suggest an alternative discipline---cooperation can
emerge from opponent-specific responses in repeated
dilemmas~\cite{morozov2026}, and remains learnable under minimal
observability~\cite{leung2026}---while Banchio, Skrzypacz and Yang show, for
auctions, that credibility must be analysed as the gap between an announced rule
and the rule the designer will actually follow~\cite{banchio2025}. I transplant
that distinction to valuation. Figure~\ref{fig:teaser} is the resulting loop;
its dashed channels are what make it strategic.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Q1---Economics.} When an evaluator can revise its rule, does
opponent-specific memory discipline disclosure the way commitment does, and at
what cost in allocative efficiency?
\item \textbf{Q2---Computation.} Can such a rule be made comparable and
auditable: encoded as a finite game, run against a fixed rule on identical
draws, and rechecked by someone who has neither the weights nor the data?
\item \textbf{Q3---Behaviour.} Is the manipulation cost that makes memory work a
real property of reporting officers, or an artefact of assuming convexity?
\end{itemize}

They are not separable. Q1's answer is a function of the cost distribution Q3
supplies; Q3's test is interpretable only inside the game Q1 specifies; and Q2
is what lets a third party check either. \textbf{Integrated question: when firms
know the metrics an AI valuation model uses, under what conditions can an
evaluator that remembers a particular firm substitute for one that commits to a
fixed rule---and when does remembering instead punish the honest?}

\section{Answer to Q1: incentives and intellectual lineage}
\textbf{Provisional answer (falsifiable).} Memory substitutes for commitment on
the \emph{deterrence} margin, not on accuracy, and only in rounds that have a
successor.

\textbf{Players, actions, payoffs, timing, information.} A firm privately knows
quality $\theta\in\{1,2,3\}$ and manipulation-cost type $k\in\{0.5,1,2\}$; an
evaluator prices; investors allocate mechanically and are not strategic. Per
round the firm picks effort $a\in\{0,1\}$ and window-dressing $m$ on a grid, and
reports $s=\theta+a+m$. A \emph{strategy} maps the private type into a pair of
actions across two rounds, which is why $m_1$ and $m_2$ can differ for one type
even though the \emph{action} set is the same in both. Payoffs: firm
$\pi=p-0.5a-km^{2}$; welfare $W=v-km^{2}$ with true value $v=\theta+a$; the
evaluator is scored by $|p-v|$. Timing: the firm reports, the evaluator posts
$p$, and only then is the outcome $y=v+\epsilon$ realised. Information: $\theta$,
$k$ and $\epsilon$ are unobserved by the evaluator; the allowance $\bar m$ is
announced. Solution concept: perfect Bayesian equilibrium in the
Bayesian-equilibrium sense Harsanyi defined~\cite{harsanyi1968}. The
course-scale computation reports type-by-type best responses against a posted
rule rather than a full fixed point; that is a limitation, stated here so it is
not read as a result.

\textbf{The two rules.} \textsc{Committed}: the announced allowance $\bar m$ is
the one followed, in every round and for every firm. \textsc{Revisable}: $\bar m$
is still announced, but after round~1 the evaluator follows
$\bar m+\lambda g_1$, where $g_1=(s_1-y_1)-\bar m$ is the residual inflation it
can infer. The wedge between announced and followed is the Banchio et al.\
object~\cite{banchio2025}, moved from auction design to valuation.

\textbf{What the benchmark settles and leaves open.} Frankel and Kartik
establish that \emph{committing} to underuse manipulable data can improve
allocation~\cite{frankel2022}; they leave open what a receiver that can retrain
should do. \textbf{Evidence status.} $\theta$, $k$, $\bar m$, $\lambda$ and
$\epsilon$ are hypothetical model primitives; every number in Section~3 is an
observed output of my own simulation; cost convexity is \emph{inherited} as
motivation from~\cite{fischbacher2013} and is not estimated; nothing is
estimated from firm data.

\section{Answer to Q2: method and evaluation}
\textbf{Inputs, algorithm, baseline, metric.} Inputs are $(\theta,k,\sigma,
\lambda)$ and the action grid. The algorithm enumerates every two-round strategy
and takes expected payoff over 20{,}000 draws of $\epsilon\sim U(-\sigma,\sigma)$.
The baseline is \textsc{committed}; the single modification is to make the rule
\textsc{revisable}. Following the instructor's Week~2 note, I record
\emph{disclosure accuracy} (mean $|p-v|$) \emph{and deviation gain}---the
expected payoff of the best strategy minus that of reporting honestly at the
same effort---alongside welfare and manipulation magnitude.

\textbf{The meaningful check.} The notebook re-implements JavaScript's
\texttt{mulberry32} PRNG in Python and reproduces all four cases logged from the
deployed browser Space: same draw ($\theta=2$, $k=0.5$), same $\epsilon$
sequence, same prices, to the cent (\texttt{verify\_space\_replication}, cell~2;
an assertion, so the run fails rather than quietly disagrees). Game and notebook
are therefore the \emph{same} model, and the page now exposes the same two knobs
the sweep varies, so a reader can reach the Section~4 threshold by hand (seed~2,
$\sigma=2$: an honest firm is priced 2.00 against a true value of 4.00, where the
committed rule says 3.00). Two scope differences remain and are not hidden. The
page draws $\epsilon$ from three points $\{-\sigma,0,+\sigma\}$ while the notebook
draws $\epsilon\sim U(-\sigma,\sigma)$, so the \emph{frequency} of mis-punishment
differs ($2/3$ versus $(\sigma-\bar m)/\sigma$) even though the threshold
$\sigma^{*}=\bar m$ does not; and the page plays one seed by hand where the
notebook averages 20{,}000 draws over the full type space.

\textbf{Obtained} (simulated outputs of my own model; not evidence about any
real firm). Averages are over all nine firm types.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Deviation gain halves} under \textsc{revisable}: $0.333\to0.167$ on
the browser grid, $0.692\to0.346$ on the research grid, and it is flat in
$\sigma$ across $[0,3]$. This is the margin on which H3 survives.
\item \emph{Deterrence stops at the horizon.} The cheap type ($k=0.5$) sets
$m_1=0$ under \textsc{revisable} but still $m_2=1$: memory disciplines every
round that has a successor, and no more.
\item \emph{Accuracy moves the other way, on every grid.} The revisable rule is
\emph{less} accurate than the committed one under both grids ($0.750$ vs $0.667$
on the browser grid, $0.869$ vs $0.571$ on the research grid), because its
round-2 price carries the outcome noise while the committed rule merely deducts
a constant. Welfare runs the opposite way on both grids ($5.833$ vs $5.667$;
$5.632$ vs $5.263$): the committed rule buys its precision with burnt real
resources---it reaches $|p-v|=0$ for the $k=0.5$ type only by inducing exactly
the inflation it allows ($m=\bar m=1$).
\item \emph{What the coarse grid does distort is the honest share.} It reports
two-thirds of types as fully honest under both rules; on the research grid no
type is ($0.000$ under both), because a finer $m$ grid always leaves a
profitable small inflation. v1 promised to report grid sensitivity rather than
hide it; this is that report, and it is why H3 is stated on deviation gain and
welfare---magnitudes that survive the grid change---rather than on a binary
honest/dishonest split, which does not.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Planned, not obtained.} A CFR fixed point in OpenSpiel~\cite{lanctot2019}
on the identical tree; horizons beyond two; heterogeneous evaluators.

\section{Answer to Q3: behaviour and competing explanations}
\textbf{Mechanism and its competitor.} The model assumes a convex, privately
known manipulation cost---the behavioural reading of die-roll evidence that
honesty is heterogeneous, with partial liars alongside honest and fully
self-serving reporters~\cite{fischbacher2013}. The competing explanation is that
reporting officers pay no such cost but reason boundedly: they do not compute
that a revisable evaluator will price the inflation out next round, so what
looks like cost is a reasoning limit~\cite{wright2020}.

\textbf{Evidence that discriminates.} The two predict opposite things about
$\lambda$. A cost story predicts \emph{saturation}: once the anticipated
punishment exceeds the gain, raising it further changes nothing. A
bounded-reasoning story predicts continued response to a more visible
punishment. The sweep shows saturation---deviation gain falls
$0.333\to0.250\to0.167$ as $\lambda$ goes $0\to0.25\to0.5$, then is flat to
$\lambda=2$---while mispricing is minimised at $\lambda=0.25$ and rises
monotonically after. \textbf{The $\lambda=1$ hard-coded in my v1 demo therefore
buys no deterrence over $\lambda=0.5$ and costs accuracy}; the accuracy-optimal
and deterrence-optimal memory strengths are both interior and are not the same.
The feasible next test is a within-subject reporting experiment that varies
$\lambda$ across rounds and elicits beliefs about the next-round price---the
design my instructor suggested pairing with Xuantong~Fu (behavioural science),
alongside Tian~Liang (economics) on the commitment side.

\textbf{Can memory punish the honest?} Yes, with a threshold. An honest firm
($m=0$) is priced below its true value in $49.9\%$ of draws for every
$\sigma>0$. But it is priced \emph{worse than under commitment} only once
$\sigma>\bar m$, with share $(\sigma-\bar m)/\sigma$: simulation returns
$0.198$, $0.331$, $0.500$, $0.668$ at $\sigma=1.25,1.5,2.0,3.0$ against an
analytic $0.200$, $0.333$, $0.500$, $0.667$. On the mean the crossing comes
later, at $\sigma=2\bar m$. So the disparate-impact risk is not everywhere: it
is bounded by the ratio of outcome noise to the announced allowance. Small and
young firms are precisely those with noisy outcomes, which makes that ratio the
quantity a regulator would need.

\textbf{Limits, and what would change my mind.} Simulated results establish
nothing about human or real AI behaviour. If $\lambda$-saturation vanished on a
finer grid, or if elicited costs proved flat rather than convex, the deterrence
result would be an artefact of my functional form and H3 would have to be
withdrawn.

\section{Advanced development and future directions}
\textbf{Foundation and enduring question.} The enduring question is trust: when
can a claim be believed by someone who cannot verify it directly? The 2001 Prize
in Economic Sciences~\cite{nobel2001} established the organising answer---costly
signals carry information under unequal information~\cite{spence1973}---and the
2007 Prize~\cite{nobel2007} made the rule itself a design object rather than a
fixed environment. The computational lineage is the 2012 ACM A.M. Turing Award
to Goldwasser and Micali~\cite{turing2012}, whose interactive and zero-knowledge
proofs established that a claim can be verified \emph{without disclosing what
makes it true}~\cite{goldwasser1989}---exactly what an outside auditor needs
from a proprietary valuation model.

\textbf{What is distinctive now, and what is unresolved.} The receiver is no
longer a market with a fixed rule; it is a model that retrains, whose announced
rule need not be the one it follows~\cite{perdomo2020,banchio2025}. Current work
leaves two things open: whether adaptation can replace commitment, and how a
third party could check either without the weights. This proposal advances that
boundary by making the two rules comparable on one tree. Economics supplies the
welfare criterion that accuracy alone misses; computation supplies the
reproducible comparison; behaviour supplies the cost that decides whether
deterrence is real. The integration yields the claim none of them holds
alone~\cite{osullivan2025}: \emph{memory substitutes for commitment on the
deterrence margin and only in non-terminal rounds, while opening a
noise-dependent false-punishment channel whose threshold can be stated.}

\textbf{Expected 2056 Nobel Prize and Turing Award contribution statement.}
By 2056 I aspire to be recognised for a design protocol that lets an outsider
test whether a proprietary valuation model rewards genuine value or cheap
reporting, without requiring the model's weights---addressing the enduring
question of trust under unequal information, in an era whose evaluators retrain
faster than they can commit. Building on Akerlof, Spence and Stiglitz (2001),
Hurwicz, Maskin and Myerson (2007), and Goldwasser and Micali (2012), I will
integrate economics (when memory substitutes for commitment), computer science
(verification without disclosure) and behavioural science (manipulation costs
elicited rather than assumed) to advance the boundary at which an adaptive
evaluator can be held accountable. Progress will be demonstrated by the
milestones in Table~\ref{tab:roadmap}, with intellectual merits in the
conditions under which adaptation and commitment are interchangeable, and
practical impacts for small firms currently priced by rules they cannot inspect.
