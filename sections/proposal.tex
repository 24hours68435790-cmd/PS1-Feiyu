\section{Three connected research questions}
Once a scored metric is a target, firms optimise the number rather than the
fundamentals behind it~\cite{strathern1997}---studied for decades in
economics~\cite{healy1999,stein1989,brown2019} and, from the other side, as
models that change the distribution they predict~\cite{perdomo2020} facing
agents who respond to the announced rule~\cite{hardt2016}. Stakeholders: firms
scored, investors allocating mechanically from the posted price, an auditor who
sees behaviour but not weights.

\textbf{The strategic tension.} An AI evaluator can retrain, so it need not
follow the rule it announces---where the 2001 Nobel foundation
stops~\cite{nobel2001}: costly signals carry information under unequal
information~\cite{spence1973}, but the receiver's rule is fixed.
Opponent-specific responses are an alternative
discipline~\cite{morozov2026,leung2026}, and Banchio et al.\ show, for auctions,
that credibility is the gap between the announced rule and the followed
one~\cite{banchio2025}. I transplant that to valuation
(Figure~\ref{fig:teaser}).

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Q1---Economics.} When an evaluator can revise its rule, does
opponent-specific memory discipline disclosure the way commitment does, and at
what cost in allocative efficiency?
\item \textbf{Q2---Computation.} Can such a rule be made comparable and
auditable---encoded as a finite game, run against a fixed rule on identical
draws, and rechecked by someone with neither the weights nor the data?
\item \textbf{Q3---Behaviour.} Is the manipulation cost that makes memory work a
real property of reporting officers, or an artefact of assuming convexity?
\end{itemize}

They are not separable: Q1 depends on the cost distribution Q3 supplies, Q3 is
interpretable only inside the game Q1 specifies, and Q2 lets a third party check
either. \textbf{Integrated question: when firms
know the metrics an AI valuation model uses, under what conditions can an
evaluator that remembers a particular firm substitute for one that commits to a
fixed rule---and when does remembering instead punish the honest?}

\section{Answer to Q1: incentives and intellectual lineage}
\textbf{Provisional answer (falsifiable).} Memory substitutes for commitment on
the \emph{deterrence} margin, not on accuracy, and only in rounds that have a
successor.

\textbf{Players, actions, payoffs, timing, information.} A firm privately knows
$\theta\in\{1,2,3\}$ and cost type $k\in\{0.5,1,2\}$; an evaluator prices;
investors allocate mechanically and are not strategic. Per round the firm picks
$a\in\{0,1\}$ and window-dressing $m$ on a grid, reporting $s=\theta+a+m$; a
\emph{strategy} maps the type into a pair of actions, which is why $m_1$ and
$m_2$ can differ. Payoffs: $\pi=p-0.5a-km^{2}$; welfare $W=v-km^{2}$ with
$v=\theta+a$; the evaluator is scored by $|p-v|$. Timing: report, then price,
then $y=v+\epsilon$. Information: $\theta$, $k$, $\epsilon$ unobserved; $\bar m$
announced. Solution concept: perfect Bayesian equilibrium in Harsanyi's
sense~\cite{harsanyi1968}. The computation reports type-by-type best responses
against a posted rule, not a full fixed point---stated so it is not read as a
result.

\textbf{The two rules.} \textsc{Committed}: the announced $\bar m$ is the one
followed, every round, every firm. \textsc{Revisable}: $\bar m$ is still
announced, but after round~1 the evaluator follows $\bar m+\lambda g_1$, where
$g_1=(s_1-y_1)-\bar m$ is the residual inflation it can infer. That wedge
between announced and followed is the Banchio et al.\ object~\cite{banchio2025},
moved from auction design to valuation.

\textbf{Benchmark and evidence status.} Frankel and Kartik show \emph{committing}
to underuse manipulable data can improve allocation~\cite{frankel2022}, leaving
open what a receiver that retrains should do. $\theta$, $k$, $\bar m$, $\lambda$,
$\epsilon$ are hypothetical primitives; every Section~3 number is an output of my
own simulation; cost convexity is inherited as motivation
from~\cite{fischbacher2013}, never estimated; nothing comes from firm data.

\section{Answer to Q2: method and evaluation}
\textbf{Inputs, algorithm, baseline, metric.} Inputs are
$(\theta,k,\sigma,\lambda)$ and the action grid. The algorithm enumerates every
two-round strategy and takes expected payoff over 20{,}000 draws of
$\epsilon\sim U(-\sigma,\sigma)$. The baseline is \textsc{committed}; the single
modification makes the rule \textsc{revisable}. Following the instructor's
Week~2 note I record \emph{disclosure accuracy} (mean $|p-v|$) \emph{and
deviation gain}---the expected payoff of the best strategy minus that of
reporting honestly at the same effort---alongside welfare.

\textbf{The meaningful check.} The notebook re-implements JavaScript's
\texttt{mulberry32} PRNG in Python and reproduces all four cases logged from the
deployed Space---same draw, same $\epsilon$ sequence, same prices, to the
cent---as an assertion, so the run fails rather than quietly disagrees. Game and
notebook are therefore the \emph{same} model, and the page exposes the same
knobs, so a reader reaches the Section~4 threshold by hand (seed~2, $\sigma=2$:
an honest firm priced 2.00 against true value 4.00, where commitment says 3.00).
One scope difference is not hidden: the page draws $\epsilon$ from three points,
the notebook from $U(-\sigma,\sigma)$, so mis-punishment \emph{frequency} differs
though the threshold $\sigma^{*}=\bar m$ does not.

\textbf{Obtained} (simulated outputs of my own model, not evidence about any real
firm; averaged over all nine types).
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Deviation gain halves} under \textsc{revisable}---$0.333\to0.167$
browser, $0.692\to0.346$ research---and is flat in $\sigma$ across $[0,3]$. This
is the margin on which H3 survives.
\item \emph{Deterrence stops at the horizon.} The cheap type sets $m_1=0$ under
\textsc{revisable} but still $m_2=1$: memory disciplines rounds with a
successor, and no more.
\item \emph{Accuracy moves the other way on every grid} ($0.750$ vs $0.667$;
$0.869$ vs $0.571$): the revisable round-2 price carries the noise while
commitment deducts a constant. Welfare runs opposite ($5.833$ vs $5.667$;
$5.632$ vs $5.263$)---commitment buys precision by inducing exactly the
inflation it allows.
\item \emph{The coarse grid distorts the honest share}---two-thirds read fully
honest under both rules against $0.000$ on the research grid, since a finer grid
always leaves a profitable small inflation. This is the grid check v1 promised
and never ran, and why H3 is stated on deviation gain and welfare.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Planned, not obtained.} A CFR fixed point in
OpenSpiel~\cite{lanctot2019} on the identical tree; horizons beyond two;
heterogeneous evaluators.

\section{Answer to Q3: behaviour and competing explanations}
\textbf{Mechanism and its competitor.} The model assumes a convex, privately
known manipulation cost---the behavioural reading of die-roll evidence that
honesty is heterogeneous~\cite{fischbacher2013}. The competitor: officers pay no
such cost but reason boundedly, failing to compute that a revisable evaluator
prices the inflation out next round, so what looks like cost is a reasoning
limit~\cite{wright2020}.

\textbf{Evidence that discriminates.} The two predict opposite things about
$\lambda$: a cost story predicts \emph{saturation} once anticipated punishment
exceeds the gain, bounded reasoning predicts continued response to more visible
punishment. The sweep shows saturation---deviation gain falls
$0.333\to0.250\to0.167$ as $\lambda$ goes $0\to0.25\to0.5$, then is flat to
$\lambda=2$---while mispricing is minimised at $\lambda=0.25$ and rises
thereafter. \textbf{The $\lambda=1$ hard-coded in my v1 demo therefore buys no
deterrence over $\lambda=0.5$ and costs accuracy}; both optima are interior and
they differ. The feasible next test is a within-subject reporting experiment
varying $\lambda$ while eliciting beliefs about the next-round price.

\textbf{Can memory punish the honest?} Yes, with a threshold. An honest firm
($m=0$) is priced below value in $49.9\%$ of draws for every $\sigma>0$, but
\emph{worse than under commitment} only once $\sigma>\bar m$, with share
$(\sigma-\bar m)/\sigma$: simulation returns $0.198$, $0.331$, $0.500$, $0.668$
at $\sigma=1.25,1.5,2.0,3.0$ against an analytic $0.200$, $0.333$, $0.500$,
$0.667$. Disparate-impact risk is therefore bounded by noise over announced
allowance---and small, young firms are precisely those with noisy outcomes,
which makes that ratio what a regulator would need.

\textbf{What would change my mind.} If $\lambda$-saturation vanished on a finer
grid, or elicited costs proved flat rather than convex, the deterrence result
would be an artefact of my functional form and H3 would have to be withdrawn.

\section{Advanced development and future directions}
\textbf{Foundation and enduring question.} The enduring question is trust: when
can a claim be believed by someone who cannot verify it? The 2001
Prize~\cite{nobel2001} gave the organising answer---costly signals carry
information under unequal information~\cite{spence1973}---and the 2007
Prize~\cite{nobel2007} made the rule itself a design object. The computational
lineage is the 2012 Turing Award to Goldwasser and Micali~\cite{turing2012},
whose zero-knowledge proofs established that a claim can be verified
\emph{without disclosing what makes it true}~\cite{goldwasser1989}---exactly
what an auditor needs from a proprietary model.

\textbf{What is distinctive now.} The receiver is a model that retrains, whose
announced rule need not be the one it follows. Open: whether adaptation replaces
commitment, and how a third party checks either without the weights. Economics
supplies the welfare criterion accuracy misses, computation the reproducible
comparison, behaviour the cost deciding whether deterrence is real. Integrated,
the claim none holds alone~\cite{osullivan2025}:
\emph{memory substitutes for commitment on the deterrence margin and only in
non-terminal rounds, while opening a noise-dependent false-punishment channel
whose threshold can be stated.}

\textbf{Expected 2056 Nobel Prize and Turing Award contribution statement.} By
2056 I aspire to be recognised for a design protocol letting an outsider test
whether a proprietary valuation model rewards genuine value or cheap reporting,
without its weights---trust under unequal information, in an era whose
evaluators retrain faster than they commit. Building on Akerlof, Spence and
Stiglitz (2001), Hurwicz, Maskin and Myerson (2007) and Goldwasser and Micali
(2012), I integrate economics (when memory substitutes for commitment), computer
science (verification without disclosure) and behavioural science (costs
elicited, not assumed) to advance the boundary at which an adaptive evaluator
can be held accountable. Milestones: Table~\ref{tab:roadmap}. Merits: the
conditions under which adaptation and commitment are interchangeable. Impact:
small firms priced by rules they cannot inspect.
