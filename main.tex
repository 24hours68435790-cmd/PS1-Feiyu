\begin{abstract}
When an AI model prices firms on their own reported metrics, the metric becomes
a target. I ask a narrow question: can an evaluator that \emph{remembers} a
particular firm substitute for one that \emph{commits} to a fixed rule? Following
the distinction Banchio et al.\ draw for auctions between an announced rule and
the rule a designer will follow, I compare a \textsc{committed} evaluator against
a \textsc{revisable} one on an identical two-period disclosure tree, identical
draws and identical firm actions, so that only the rule differs. A Python twin of
the deployed browser Space reproduces its logged prices to the cent, then sweeps
what a browser cannot. Memory halves the gain from inflating but only in rounds
that have a successor, and it prices an honest firm below value in half of all
noisy draws---becoming worse than commitment once outcome noise exceeds the
announced allowance. All results are simulated outputs of my own model.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{strategic disclosure, performative prediction, mechanism design,
credible rules, imperfect-information games, AI valuation, Goodhart's law}
